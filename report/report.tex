\begin{abstract}
Environmental sound recognition (ESR) is essential for autonomous acoustic monitoring, yet out-of-distribution acoustic corruptions severely degrade real-world performance. In this work, we present a systematic multi-label benchmarking study on FSD50K under both clean and heavily corrupted acoustic conditions. We evaluate Convolutional Neural Network baselines (ResNet-18/34, EfficientNet-B0/B4) against Audio Spectrogram Transformers (ViT-Tiny/Small). While convolutional architectures like EfficientNet-B4 achieve the strongest clean precision ($0.5396$ mAP) and parameter efficiency, Vision Transformers provide competitive discrimination and Top-1 retrieval ($76.54\%$ for ViT-Small). Under unseen $-5\text{ dB}$ SNR noise corruption, undefended ViT-Tiny performance degrades from $0.5068$ to $0.2040$ mAP. To address this degradation, we develop an acoustic augmentation pipeline combining continuous spectral-slope colored noise ($1/f^\alpha$, $\alpha \in [-2, 2]$), Schroeder room reverberation, Mixup ($\alpha=0.5$), and learned attention pooling. The resulting robust model improves $-5\text{ dB}$ performance to $0.3182$ mAP ($+56.0\%$ relative gain over baseline ViT-Tiny) while reaching $0.5303$ clean mAP without external noise datasets. Finally, extensive ablations quantify the trade-offs of token pooling heads, decision threshold calibration, and individual augmentation components.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} Environmental Sound Recognition, Audio Spectrogram Transformer, Noise Robustness, Digital Signal Processing Augmentation, FSD50K, Multi-Label Classification.

\section{Introduction}
Environmental Sound Classification (ESC) and multi-label Sound Event Recognition (SER) aim to identify and categorize sound events occurring in unstructured acoustic environments. Unlike human speech or Western musical compositions, environmental sounds are highly non-stationary, exhibit diverse spectral-temporal morphology (ranging from sharp acoustic transients to broadband ambient hums), and frequently overlap in polyphonic mixtures.

\subsection{The Real-World Acoustic Domain Gap}
While deep learning models have achieved impressive recognition benchmarks on curated, clean audio datasets such as FSD50K~\cite{fonseca2021fsd50k} and AudioSet~\cite{gemmeke2017audioset}, real-world acoustic deployments present a severe domain shift. In applications such as Internet of Things (IoT) acoustic monitoring, hearing prosthetics, and autonomous navigation, incoming acoustic signals are continuously contaminated by ambient background noise (traffic, HVAC systems, wind, crowds). Most existing academic benchmarks evaluate models exclusively on identically distributed clean splits, obscuring the catastrophic failure modes that occur under adverse Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNR).

\subsection{Research Questions}
To systematically bridge this gap, this investigation is structured around four concrete Research Questions (RQs):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Architectural Inductive Bias):} How do modern Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures compare against standard Convolutional Neural Networks (ResNet, EfficientNet) in multi-label environmental sound classification regarding recognition accuracy (mAP as primary metric, Top-1 hit and mAUC secondary), parameter efficiency, and computational cost?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Token Pooling Representations):} Given that Vision Transformers produce hundreds of spatial patch tokens over an audio spectrogram, how do different pooling mechanisms (Dual-Token $\text{CLS}+\text{DIST}$ pooling vs. Global Average Pooling vs. Attention Pooling) affect multi-label sound discrimination?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Acoustic Noise Robustness):} How severely does model performance degrade under varying signal-to-noise ratios (SNR from $+10\text{ dB}$ down to $-5\text{ dB}$), and can purely synthetic DSP augmentations (continuous colored noise, Schroeder reverberation) induce acoustic invariance without relying on external noise data?
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (Augmentation Component Contributions):} What are the individual and synergistic contributions of time-masking, frequency-masking, and Mixup in regularizing multi-label spectrogram classification, and how does decision threshold calibration resolve tail-class recall compression?
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{CNNs vs. Transformers in Audio Classification}
Historically, deep audio recognition relied on 2D Convolutional Neural Networks applied to time-frequency representations (Mel-spectrograms). Architectures such as PANNs (CNN-14)~\cite{kong2020panns} and ResNet~\cite{he2016deep} treat spectrograms as single-channel images, leveraging local receptive fields and translation equivariance.

Recently, the Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST)~\cite{gong2021ast} adapted Vision Transformers (ViT) and Data-efficient Image Transformers (DeiT)~\cite{touvron2021training} to audio. By dividing spectrograms into overlapping 2D patches and employing multi-head self-attention, AST captures non-local temporal-spectral dependencies across distant frequency bands (such as fundamental pitches and harmonic overtones).

\subsection{Data Augmentation in Audio}
Spectrogram data augmentation is essential for regularizing high-capacity neural networks. SpecAugment~\cite{park2019specaugment} applies random blocks of frequency masking and time masking, preventing models from over-fitting to localized spectral artifacts. Mixup~\cite{zhang2018mixup} performs convex combinations of audio pairs and their corresponding multi-hot ground truth labels:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \lambda \mathbf{x}_i + (1 - \lambda) \mathbf{x}_j, \quad \tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \lambda \mathbf{y}_i + (1 - \lambda) \mathbf{y}_j
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$. In multi-label sound event recognition, Mixup naturally reflects the physical principle of acoustic wave superposition.

\subsection{Robustness and Acoustic Corruption Benchmarks}
Although speech recognition has standardized noise benchmarks (such as NOISEX-92 and the ACE Corpus~\cite{eaton2016ace}), open-domain environmental sound recognition lacks standardized out-of-distribution noise evaluation pipelines. Room reverberation modeling via statistical impulse responses~\cite{schroeder1962natural} offers a principled, physics-informed mechanism to simulate reverberant propagation. Prior works often report accuracy exclusively on clean test splits, failing to quantify model robustness when tested under real acoustic degradation.

\section{Dataset and Acoustic Environment Benchmark}

\subsection{Primary Dataset: FSD50K}
We conduct all primary model training and validation on FSD50K~\cite{fonseca2021fsd50k} (Version 1.0), an open dataset of human-labeled sound events drawn from the AudioSet Ontology:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Official URL:} \url{https://zenodo.org/record/4060432}
    \item \textbf{Vocabulary:} 200 sound classes hierarchically organized into musical instruments, human sounds, domestic sounds, and environmental soundscapes.
    \item \textbf{Split Partitions:} Standard evaluation split consisting of a Development set (36,796 training clips, $\sim 80$ hours; 4,170 validation clips, $\sim 12$ hours) and an Evaluation test set (10,231 clips, $\sim 24$ hours).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Acoustic Noise Benchmark: ACE Dataset}
To establish a rigorous out-of-distribution evaluation benchmark without contaminating training splits, we utilize the Acoustic Characterization of Environments (ACE) corpus~\cite{eaton2016ace} (\url{http://ee.ic.ac.uk/naylor/ACEweb/index.html}). The ACE corpus provides high-fidelity ambient background noises recorded across diverse indoor acoustic spaces (offices, lecture halls, meeting rooms) containing genuine noise sources such as HVAC ventilation fans, ambient human speech babble, and computer cooling equipment. We construct a standardized out-of-distribution evaluation suite by mixing the 10,231 clean held-out FSD50K evaluation clips with calibrated ACE ambient noises across four standardized Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) tiers: $+10\text{ dB}$ (mild interference), $+5\text{ dB}$ (moderate interference), $0\text{ dB}$ (equal power), and $-5\text{ dB}$ (heavy noise corruption), resulting in $40,924$ corrupted evaluation instances.

\subsection{Audio Preprocessing Pipeline}
All audio signals are standardized to $16,000\text{ Hz}$ mono PCM audio. Clips are framed into fixed $10.0\text{ s}$ windows ($160,000$ samples), using random cropping during training and center cropping during validation/testing. Clips shorter than $10.0\text{ s}$ are zero-padded.

Log-Mel spectrograms are extracted using Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Window length: $25\text{ ms}$ ($400$ samples), Hop length: $10\text{ ms}$ ($160$ samples).
    \item Mel filterbank: $N_{\text{mels}} = 128$ bins covering $0\text{ Hz}$ to $8,000\text{ Hz}$.
    \item Logarithmic scaling: $S(t, f) = \log(\text{Mel}(t, f) + 10^{-6})$.
    \item Global standardization: $\tilde{S} = \frac{S - \mu}{2\sigma}$ with global dataset statistics ($\mu = -4.2677$, $\sigma = 4.5690$). Target input tensor shape is $1 \times 128 \times 1000$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Convolutional Baseline Architectures vs. Audio Spectrogram Transformers}
\label{sec:cnn_baselines}
Audio spectrograms represent acoustic energy across continuous time (horizontal axis) and frequency (vertical axis). Treating a log-Mel spectrogram as an optical image introduces fundamental physical discrepancies that differentiate Convolutional Neural Networks from Vision Transformers.

In optical computer vision, 2D convolution leverages spatial translation equivariance: an object retains its identity regardless of spatial coordinates. In audio spectrograms, the temporal axis is translation-invariant (a sound event occurring at $t=2\text{ s}$ vs. $t=6\text{ s}$ retains its category), but the frequency axis is \textbf{strictly non-shift-invariant}. Shifting spectral energy upward alters pitch, formant structure, and harmonic intervals, fundamentally changing sound identity. Standard CNNs (ResNet-18/34, EfficientNet-B0/B4) apply small $3 \times 3$ convolutional kernels that excel at localized spectro-temporal textures and onset bursts, but require deep layer hierarchies and spatial pooling to capture multi-octave harmonic coherence (e.g., fundamental pitches at $100\text{ Hz}$ and integer overtones at $1\text{--}4\text{ kHz}$).

In contrast, the Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) divides the 2D spectrogram into patches and applies multi-head self-attention. This establishes an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ information path length between any pair of time-frequency patches, allowing early self-attention layers to directly attend to simultaneous harmonic overtones across the entire 128 Mel channels. Learnable positional embeddings encode both temporal offset and frequency band index, capturing non-shift-invariant spectral structures naturally. To transfer visual pre-training without RGB channel expansion, the initial convolutional layer (\texttt{conv1}) of ResNet and EfficientNet is adapted from 3 channels to 1 single spectrogram channel by averaging pre-trained weights across the channel dimension ($\mathbf{W}_{\text{conv1\_new}} = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{c=1}^3 \mathbf{W}_{\text{orig}}[:, c, :, :]$), preserving pre-activation variance entering the subsequent batch normalization layer.

\subsection{Main Proposed Method: Audio Spectrogram Transformer}
Our primary classification architecture is the Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) built upon DeiT ViT-Tiny and ViT-Small backbones:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Patch Tokenization:} The 2D spectrogram $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times 1000}$ is divided into $P \times P$ patches ($16 \times 16$) with a stride of 10 pixels (an overlap of $6$ pixels in both time and frequency), producing $N = 1,188$ patch tokens.
    \item \textbf{Linear Projection and Positional Embeddings:} Each patch is flattened and linearly projected into dimension $D$ ($D=192$ for ViT-Tiny, $D=384$ for ViT-Small). Following standard AST~\cite{gong2021ast}, the pre-trained DeiT 3-channel patch projection weights are adapted to single-channel spectrograms by summing across the channel dimension ($\mathbf{W}_{\text{patch\_1ch}} = \sum_{c=1}^3 \mathbf{W}_{\text{patch\_3ch}}[:, c, :, :]$). Unlike convolutional layers followed by immediate normalization, ViT adds the linear projection directly to $\mathbf{E}_{\text{pos}}$; channel summation preserves the relative embedding magnitude between projected patch content and pre-trained positional embeddings:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{z}_0 = [\mathbf{x}_{\text{CLS}}; \mathbf{x}_{\text{DIST}}; \mathbf{E}\mathbf{x}_p^1; \dots; \mathbf{E}\mathbf{x}_p^N] + \mathbf{E}_{\text{pos}}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{x}_{\text{CLS}}$ and $\mathbf{x}_{\text{DIST}}$ are learnable classification and distillation tokens, and $\mathbf{E}_{\text{pos}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+2) \times D}$ represents 1D learnable position embeddings initialized from DeiT and interpolated to $1,190$ tokens.
    \item \textbf{Self-Attention Encoder:} The sequence is processed through 12 standard Transformer encoder blocks containing Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) and MLP sub-layers with LayerNorm and residual connections.
    \item \textbf{Classification Head:} The output representation is pooled and projected onto the 200 target classes:
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{W}_{\text{cls}} \cdot \text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{h}) + \mathbf{b}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Token Pooling Mechanisms}
We explore four distinct patch aggregation strategies over the output tokens $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{1188 \times D}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dual-Token Pooling (Baseline):} Average of the classification and distillation tokens: $\mathbf{h} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{CLS}} + \mathbf{z}_{\text{DIST}})$.
    \item \textbf{Global Average Pooling (GAP):} Uniform mean over all $1,188$ patch tokens: $\mathbf{h} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{z}_i$.
    \item \textbf{GAP + Max Pooling:} Concatenation of mean and peak patch representations: $\mathbf{h} = [\text{mean}(\mathbf{Z}); \text{max}(\mathbf{Z})]$ (dimension $2D$).
    \item \textbf{Attention Pooling:} Softmax-weighted aggregation with learned query vector $\mathbf{w}_a \in \mathbb{R}^D$:
    \begin{equation}
        \alpha_i = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{w}_a^T \mathbf{z}_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(\mathbf{w}_a^T \mathbf{z}_j)}, \quad \mathbf{h} = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i \mathbf{z}_i
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Augmentation Techniques}
\label{sec:data_augmentations}
To regularize training and simulate complex acoustic scenes, we evaluate four standard data augmentation methods:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Time Masking:} Zeroes out contiguous temporal frames across all frequency bands ($\Delta t \sim \mathcal{U}(0, T_{\text{mask}})$, up to $T_{\text{mask}} = 192$ frames / $1.92\text{ s}$).
    \item \textbf{Frequency Masking:} Zeroes out contiguous frequency channels across all time steps ($\Delta f \sim \mathcal{U}(0, F_{\text{mask}})$, up to $F_{\text{mask}} = 48$ mel bins).
    \item \textbf{SpecAugment:} The joint application of both Time Masking and Frequency Masking directly on the log-mel spectrogram.
    \item \textbf{Mixup:} Convex linear interpolation of raw audio waveforms and multi-label targets: $\tilde{x} = \lambda x_a + (1 - \lambda) x_b$ and $\tilde{y} = \lambda y_a + (1 - \lambda) y_b$, where $\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_{\text{mix}}, \alpha_{\text{mix}})$ with $\alpha_{\text{mix}} = 0.5$. In audio, Mixup physically reflects acoustic wave superposition.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Proposed DSP-Augmented Robust Training Framework}
\label{sec:dsp_augmentations}
Real-world acoustic environments present severe out-of-distribution background noise and reverberation that cause undefended models to collapse. Rather than relying on external, static noise datasets that introduce dataset bias and memory overhead, we propose a purely algorithmic Digital Signal Processing (DSP) augmentation framework that synthesizes realistic acoustic corruptions directly on-the-fly.

\subsubsection{Continuous Spectral-Slope Colored Noise \texorpdfstring{($1/f^\alpha$)}{(1/f\textasciicircum alpha)}}
Physical environmental noise (wind, traffic, ventilation fans) rarely exhibits a flat white-noise spectrum. Natural acoustic ambient spectra follow power-law decays $S(f) \propto 1/f^\alpha$, where $\alpha=0$ represents white noise, $\alpha=1$ represents pink noise, and $\alpha=2$ represents brown/Brownian noise. During training, we synthesize continuous spectral-slope colored noise dynamically on-the-fly:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parameter Sampling:} For each audio clip, we sample the continuous spectral slope parameter $\alpha \sim \mathcal{U}(0.0, 2.0)$ and target Signal-to-Noise Ratio $\gamma_{\text{dB}} \sim \mathcal{U}(-5.0, 20.0)\text{ dB}$ with probability $p=0.5$.
    \item \textbf{Spectral Filtering:} Standard white Gaussian noise $w(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ of length $L = 160,000$ samples ($10.0\text{ s}$ at $16\text{ kHz}$) is transformed to the frequency domain $W(f) = \mathcal{F}\{w(t)\}$. We apply a continuous frequency-domain transfer function $H(f)$:
    \begin{equation}
        H(f) = \frac{1}{\max(f, f_{\text{min}})^{\alpha/2}}, \quad f_{\text{min}} = 20\text{ Hz}
    \end{equation}
    where $f_{\text{min}} = 20\text{ Hz}$ avoids singular division at DC ($f=0$).
    \item \textbf{Calibrated Additive Mixing:} The colored noise is transformed back to the time domain via inverse FFT $n_{\text{raw}}(t) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{W(f) \cdot H(f)\}$, normalized to unit Root-Mean-Square (RMS) energy, and scaled to match the target SNR $\gamma_{\text{dB}}$ relative to clean audio $x(t)$:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{RMS}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^L x(t)^2}, \quad \sigma_{\text{noise}} = \frac{\text{RMS}(x)}{10^{\gamma_{\text{dB}}/20}}
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        x_{\text{aug}}(t) = x(t) + \sigma_{\text{noise}} \cdot \frac{n_{\text{raw}}(t)}{\text{RMS}(n_{\text{raw}}(t))}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Schroeder Room Reverberation Modeling}
Physical acoustic propagation in enclosed spaces generates early reflections and dense decaying reverberant tails. To simulate realistic acoustic environments without pre-recorded Room Impulse Responses (RIRs), we implement Schroeder's statistical reverberator model~\cite{schroeder1962natural}:
\begin{equation}
    h(t) = e^{-\frac{6.9078 \cdot t}{T_{60}}} \cdot v(t), \quad 0 \le t \le T_{\text{max}}, \quad v(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
\end{equation}
where $T_{60} \sim \mathcal{U}(0.15, 0.60)\text{ s}$ denotes the reverberation decay time (time required for energy to decay by $60\text{ dB}$), $T_{\text{max}} = 1.0\text{ s}$ ($16,000$ samples), and $v(t)$ is Gaussian white noise modeling diffuse reflections. With probability $p=0.3$, the impulse response is convolved via fast FFT circular convolution $x_{\text{reverb}}(t) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\mathcal{F}\{x(t)\} \cdot \mathcal{F}\{h(t)\}\}$ and normalized to match the RMS energy of $x(t)$.

\subsubsection{Proposed End-to-End System Specifications}
Our final robust architecture integrates these components into a unified system (Figure~\ref{fig:final_model_architecture}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Backbone Encoder:} ViT-Tiny backbone ($16 \times 16$ patches, stride 10, $N=1,188$ tokens, $D=192$, 12 layers, $5.58\text{M}$ parameters, $2.98\text{ GMACs}$, $6.4\text{ ms}$ latency).
    \item \textbf{Classification Head:} Learned Attention Pooling ($\mathbf{w}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{192}$) with softmax weighting over all 1,188 patch tokens, projecting linearly to 200 classes.
    \item \textbf{Streamlined Augmentation Recipe:} Pairwise Mixup ($\text{Beta}(0.5, 0.5)$, $p=0.5$), continuous colored noise ($\alpha \in [0, 2]$, $p=0.5$), Schroeder reverberation ($T_{60} \in [0.15, 0.60]\text{ s}$, $p=0.3$), time shift ($\pm 10$ frames), and class-balanced sampling (\texttt{Distributed}\-\texttt{Weighted}\-\texttt{Sampler}). SpecAugment is disabled ($\text{FM}=0, \text{TM}=0$) to prevent destructive over-masking when combined with waveform DSP noise.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison Strategy and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:comparison_strategy}
To ensure fair and rigorous benchmarking across distinct architectural families, we establish standardized evaluation protocols:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standardized Splits:} All models are evaluated on the official, held-out FSD50K evaluation split ($10,231$ clips, $\sim 24.1$ hours). Out-of-distribution robustness is benchmarked on the $40,924$ synthesized ACE noise evaluation instances across four SNR tiers (+10~dB, +5~dB, 0~dB, -5~dB).
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Metrics:} Our primary benchmark metric is Mean Average Precision (mAP), computing the macro-averaged Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (PR-AUC) across all 200 classes:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{mAP} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^C \sum_{k=1}^K P_c(k) \Delta R_c(k)
    \end{equation}
    Because multi-label environmental audio is sparse ($>99.3\%$ negative class priors), PR-AUC prioritizes true positive discrimination while penalizing false positives. Secondary metrics include Top-1 Hit Accuracy, calibrated Micro-F1 / Macro-F1 at operational deployment threshold $\tau = 0.20$, and Mean Area Under ROC Curve (mAUC).
    \item \textbf{Optimization Regimes:} Both CNN and Transformer architectures are trained for 30 epochs with AdamW ($\beta_1=0.9, \beta_2=0.999$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), multi-label BCEWithLogitsLoss, label smoothing ($0.1$), and the \texttt{ast\_step} decay schedule (5 epochs constant initial LR, then $0.90\times$ decay per epoch). Following standard practice in AST~\cite{gong2021ast} and CMKD~\cite{gong2022cmkd}, base learning rates are set to $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for CNNs and $5 \times 10^{-5}$ for Transformers. Learning rates were maintained at these established literature defaults without exhaustive per-family grid sweeps, which is noted as a practical constraint.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Ablation Study 2: Decision Threshold Sweeping and Probability Calibration}
\label{sec:threshold_sweeping}
In standard multi-label pipelines, a default decision threshold of $\tau = 0.50$ is conventionally assumed. However, this assumption is mismatched for open-domain audio: (1) each 10-second clip contains an average of only $1.3$ ground-truth labels out of 200 classes ($>99.3\%$ true negatives); and (2) training with multi-label BCE, label smoothing, and Mixup naturally compresses predicted sigmoid probabilities into $[0.15, 0.40]$. Evaluating at $\tau = 0.50$ incurs a severe recall penalty ($52.0\%\text{--}55.1\%$).

Systematic sweeping across $\tau \in [0.05, 0.90]$ on validation clips (full trajectory in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:threshold_sweep}) reveals that lowering $\tau$ from $0.50$ to $0.30$ improves Micro-F1 from $0.6517 \to 0.6695$ ($+1.8$ points). For unweighted Macro-F1, lowering $\tau$ to $0.25$ provides dramatic recovery ($0.4790 \to 0.5454$, $+6.6$ points for Mixup, and $+8.4$ points for Full Augmentation from $0.4347 \to 0.5185$). For held-out evaluation in Table~\ref{tab:setup1_architectures}, we standardize on $\tau = 0.20$, guaranteeing balanced operational recall ($>67.6\%$) while maintaining strong precision ($>60.6\%$).

\section{Discussion and Qualitative Error Analysis}

\subsection{Architectural Trade-offs and Inductive Biases}
Our empirical findings reveal complementary strengths across convolutional and Transformer paradigms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Convolutional Efficiency:} Inverted residual networks (EfficientNet-B0 and EfficientNet-B4) demonstrate superior compute-to-accuracy efficiency on clean spectrograms, with EfficientNet-B4 achieving highest clean precision ($0.5396$ mAP, $0.6114$ F1) and EfficientNet-B0 providing extreme efficiency ($0.5233$ mAP at $0.80\text{ GMACs}$). Depthwise separable convolutions efficiently capture compact, local spectro-temporal textures. Under undefended noise, EfficientNet architectures exhibit the strongest resilience ($-51.0\%$ relative drop for B0), whereas ResNet baselines degrade similarly to ViT-Small ($-54.5\%\text{ to }-56.1\%$ drop).
    \item \textbf{Self-Attention and Global Receptive Fields:} Audio Spectrogram Transformers provide competitive discriminability ($0.5068\text{--}0.5288$ clean mAP), high distribution ranking ($0.8934\text{--}0.9106$ mAUC), and strong Top-1 retrieval ($76.54\%$ for ViT-Small), hypothesized to benefit from global multi-head self-attention across distant frequency bands.
    \item \textbf{Token Pooling Flexibility:} ViT's patch tokenization allows dynamic learned attention pooling to selectively emphasize active acoustic events, boosting clean test mAP by $+0.013$ ($0.5068 \to 0.5199$) and providing an effective substrate for acoustic noise training.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Failure Mode and Root-Cause Analysis}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Failure Mode 1: Low-Energy Impulsive Events in High Noise:} Short transient events suffer severe energy masking when additive continuous noise or artificial frequency blocks match or exceed event amplitude. For \texttt{Doorbell} and \texttt{Frying\_(food)}, energy is concentrated in narrow spectro-temporal slices, causing masking to obscure transient bursts and degrading recall.
    \item \textbf{Failure Mode 2: Acoustic Similarity and Textural Confusion:} Classes sharing physical resonator properties or rhythmic impulse patterns exhibit mutual false positive confusion. For example, mechanical strikes in \texttt{Typewriter} are confused with typing and keyboard clatter, while rhythmic pulses in \texttt{Printer} are frequently misattributed.
    \item \textbf{Failure Mode 3: Continuous Ambient Noise vs. Stationary Events:} Sound classes whose intrinsic acoustics resemble stationary noise (e.g., \texttt{Wind}, \texttt{Mechanical\_fan}) possess broadband $1/f^\alpha$ power spectra. Under heavy indoor room background noise (such as HVAC fans in the ACE benchmark), false positive triggers for \texttt{Wind} increase; self-attention heads struggle to separate continuous low-frequency ventilation hum from natural atmospheric wind without explicit spatial cues.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
We highlight four methodological limitations of this study:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compound Recipe in the Robust Model:} The proposed robust model combines four distinct elements: continuous colored noise, Schroeder reverberation, learned attention pooling, and class-balanced sampling (\texttt{DistributedWeightedSampler}), while disabling SpecAugment. Although the matched attention-pooling comparison in Table~\ref{tab:dsp_noise_gain} isolates a $+49.9\%$ relative gain ($0.2123 \to 0.3182$) specifically attributable to the acoustic physical noise simulation, the sampler and masking changes remain joint factors in the overall $+56.0\%$ improvement over the dual-token baseline.
    \item \textbf{Single-Seed Evaluations:} Due to compute budget constraints on FSD50K, experiments were conducted with a single random seed ($42$). While coarse patterns (e.g. $+56\%$ noise gain) decisively exceed single-seed evaluation variance ($\sim 0.015$), fine-grained differences ($<0.010$) should be interpreted as empirical tendencies.
    \item \textbf{Architecture-Specific Learning Rate Tuning:} Learning rates were maintained at established AST and CMKD defaults ($5 \times 10^{-5}$ for Transformers, $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for CNNs) without exhaustive per-architecture grid searches, representing a practical constraint.
    \item \textbf{Acoustic Domain Scope:} The ACE evaluation benchmark comprises indoor room ambient noises (offices, lecture halls, HVAC fans, babble). While directly representative of indoor acoustic monitoring, outdoor soundscapes (turbulent open-air wind shear, high-speed traffic) involve complex acoustic dispersion patterns that warrant future outdoor benchmarking.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Answers to Research Questions}
This investigation presented a systematic empirical benchmark of multi-label environmental sound recognition on FSD50K under clean and adverse acoustic conditions. Our findings provide direct answers to the four core research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ1 (Architectural Inductive Bias):} Inverted residual convolutional networks (EfficientNet-B4 at $0.5396$ mAP, EfficientNet-B0 at $0.5233$ mAP) deliver the highest clean precision and parameter efficiency, while retaining the highest resilience under undefended noise ($-51.0\%$ relative drop at $-5\text{ dB}$). Audio Spectrogram Transformers provide competitive discrimination ($0.5068\text{--}0.5288$ clean mAP), high ranking separation ($0.8934\text{--}0.9106$ mAUC), and strong Top-1 retrieval ($76.54\%$ for ViT-Small), hypothesized to benefit from global attention over spectral patches.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ2 (Token Pooling Mechanisms):} Patch aggregation is pivotal for Transformers. Learned Attention Pooling ($0.5199$ Clean Test mAP, $76.31\%$ Top-1) outperforms unweighted GAP ($0.5126$) and rigid Dual-Token pooling ($0.5068$) by dynamically weighting active acoustic events and suppressing background silence.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ3 (Noise Robustness \& Acoustic Augmentation):} Undefended Transformers suffer severe collapse under unseen acoustic noise (falling by $59.7\%$ relative mAP to $0.2040$ at $-5\text{ dB}$ SNR). Our compound acoustic augmentation pipeline (continuous colored noise, Schroeder reverberation, Mixup, and attention pooling) establishes strong acoustic resilience, achieving $0.3182$ mAP at $-5\text{ dB}$ ($+56.0\%$ relative gain over baseline ViT-Tiny, and $+49.9\%$ over the matched attention-pool model) and boosting clean evaluation to $0.5303$ mAP without external noise data.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ4 (Augmentation Synergy \& Threshold Calibration):} Isolated spectral masking yields negligible gain on clean audio ($\le 0.23\%$ delta), whereas Mixup provides genuine regularization ($+1.15\%$ mAP, $+1.48\%$ Top-1). Heavy compound augmentation induces tail-class recall compression under default $\tau=0.50$, which is effectively resolved by lowering decision thresholds to $\tau \in [0.20, 0.25]$ (improving Macro-F1 by $+6.6$ to $+8.4$ points).
\end{itemize}
